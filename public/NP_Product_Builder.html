New Products  ·  Builder Persona

What is the Product Builder?

The software development lifecycle is being fundamentally restructured. AI agents are collapsing the walls between roles. A new organizational unit of one is emerging — and it changes everything about how tools get built, bought, and used.

The Core Thesis

Everyone Ships Now

PMs are shipping code. Marketers are running experiments. Designers are prototyping working software. At Ramp, 57% of PRs ship via coding agents. At Stripe, agents merge thousands of PRs. At Coinbase, PMs build their own coding agent harnesses to manage multiple workstreams.

The job title of "software engineer" is starting to go away — replaced by Builder. A builder is anyone who has collapsed the traditional EPD handoff into a single continuous loop they can run themselves. They have taste (can identify what's worth building), context (enough technical understanding to execute or direct an agent), and agency (organizational permission and personal drive to ship without waiting).

"By end of year, everyone's gonna be a product manager and everyone codes. The title software engineer is gonna start to go away; it's just gonna be replaced by builder."

— Boris Cherny, Head of Claude Code, on Lenny's Podcast

The builder is most accurately described as someone who has internalized the full product development loop: discover → decide → build → measure → improve. Previously, that loop required four or five people with four or five different tools. Now it requires one person and a good set of agents.

The Archetypes

The Builder Is Not One Person — It's Six

The builder persona spans at least six distinct archetypes, each with different tools, workflows, and needs. Treating them as one person will produce mediocre product decisions.

Engineering

The Engineering Builder

"I write code. I also own the outcome."

Reviews agent-generated PRs in the morning, directs agents on new features by midday, writes complex logic themselves when the agent isn't good enough. Their job isn't coding — it's taste, architecture, and signal. Needs: Low-friction SDK, CLI-first flag management, session replay for debugging, experiment results in their code review flow.

Product

The Product Builder

"I used to write specs. Now I ship features."

A PM with Cursor can ship a feature. The spec IS the prompt. The acceptance criteria IS the experiment. The product builder has collapsed two to three handoff points and runs at 2–3× the throughput of a traditional PM. Needs: AI-powered experiment setup, no-code guides, session replay for stakeholder demos.

Growth

The Growth Builder

"I run experiments like a scientist and ship landing pages like a developer."

Uses tools like Lovable, Replit, and v0 to spin up new experiences. Runs dozens of LP tests simultaneously with agent direction. Thinks in conversion rates, activation funnels, and experiment velocity. Needs: Near-zero-code web experiments, guides for in-product context, AI feedback for the qualitative "why."

Marketing

The Marketing Builder

"I used to write copy. Now I'm deploying to prod."

The most surprising archetype. Uses Claude Code for automations, Lovable for microsites, AI agents for campaign pipelines. Their superpower is storytelling combined with speed. Needs: Marketer-friendly web experiments (no SDK required), retention campaigns via guides, a dashboard they can interpret without an analyst.

Design

The Design Builder

"I used to hand off Figma files. Now I ship prototypes that work."

Designers with v0 or Lovable can build working prototypes by lunch. They care intensely about what users see and feel, and advocate for better UX observability. Needs: Session replay with heatmaps and zone analysis, AI feedback for qualitative sentiment, guides for in-product validation.

Data

The Data Builder

"I used to make charts. Now I make features that respond to data."

An analyst or data scientist who has crossed the line into execution. They build data products, self-improving features, and agent-driven feedback loops. Needs: Programmatic API/MCP access for agents, feature flags for data-driven rollouts, experiment analysis that integrates with notebooks.

The Workflow

The Builder's Loop — Not a Handoff Chain

The key insight: the builder's workflow is a loop, not a handoff chain. Traditional enterprise software was built for handoffs. The builder needs tools built for loops. Their day is a continuous cycle of signal intake, decision-making, shipping, and measurement.

Phase 1
Sense
What's different today? What's broken? What's working?
Phase 2
Decide
Direct agents on 2–3 tasks based on signal
Phase 3
Build
Ship a flag change, guide, experiment, or fix
Phase 4
Measure
Instrument it. Track the result. Watch sessions.
Phase 5
Improve
Did it work? What should the agent do tomorrow?

Every product Amplitude builds maps to one or more phases of this loop. The vision is not seven separate products — it's a connected platform where signal flows naturally from sensing to acting to learning.

Motivations

What Builders Are Optimizing For

Builders are not optimizing for the same things as traditional software buyers. Understanding their motivations is critical for both product and marketing decisions.

  • 1
    Velocity
    The builder's primary motivation is shipping speed. They are measured on output, not process. Every tool that makes them faster gets adopted. Every tool that requires a meeting to use gets abandoned.
  • 2
    Craft
    Good builders care about quality — not just speed. They want to ship things that actually work, that users love. This is why signal matters so much: they need to know whether what they shipped worked.
  • 3
    Autonomy
    Builders resist process overhead. They adopted agent tools precisely because those tools don't require a Jira ticket, a sprint planning meeting, or a manager approval.
  • 4
    Status
    In builder cultures, shipping is the currency of respect. A builder who ships ten things a week is more respected than one who ships one thing after twelve planning meetings.
  • 5
    Learning
    Builders reward tools that help them learn — about their users, about what works, about their domain. "Amplitude on Amplitude" type content is the kind of content builders share.
The Gaps

The Builder's Biggest Unmet Needs

Across all six archetypes and all signal gathered, a consistent set of unmet needs emerges. These are the gaps Amplitude's product portfolio needs to close.

  • 1
    The "Zero to Useful" Gap
    Getting started with Amplitude is too hard. Builders want to be instrumenting events within minutes of signup. The ambition: a builder instruments their first event, sees it in Amplitude, and runs their first experiment — all before their coffee gets cold.
  • 2
    The "Act on Signal" Gap
    Amplitude is excellent at showing what happened. It is not yet excellent at telling builders what to do about it. The opportunity: an AI layer that connects anomalies to probable causes, surfaces relevant session replays, and suggests next actions.
  • 3
    The "Agent-Native Workflow" Gap
    Builders run their work through Cursor, Claude Code, and similar tools. Amplitude is not yet a first-class citizen in those workflows. The vision: "pull up Amplitude signal on the checkout flow" from any coding context.
  • 4
    The "Low-Code Experience" Gap
    Builders who aren't engineers need to create in-product experiences — guides, surveys, experiment variants — without filing a Jira ticket. Describe the experience in natural language, AI generates it.
  • 5
    The "Qual + Quant Loop" Gap
    Builders need both "what is happening" and "why is it happening." Session Replay and AI Feedback exist — but they're not yet telling a unified story. When conversion drops 12%, surface the funnel, the replays, AND the synthesized user feedback.
The Two-Persona Model

Not One Builder — Two Distinct Buyers

The "builder" framing is too broad to win with one narrative. There are two distinct builders with different buying triggers, evaluation criteria, competitive sets, and relationships with the products we build.

Persona 1: The Engineer. Shipping code is the job. They evaluate tools on SDK quality, time to first flag, stats engine trust, and observability integration. Their competitive set is LaunchDarkly, Statsig, and Datadog. They live in the terminal and their IDE.

Persona 2: Everyone Else (PM, Marketer, Growth). Shipping code is increasingly part of the job — but not the whole job. They evaluate tools on time to insight, no-code UX, funnel clarity, and whether results are trustworthy enough to present to leadership. Their competitive set is PostHog, Pendo, Optimizely.

"When you build for 'builders' generically, you risk building a product that's too technical for PMs and too product-oriented for engineers. The two-persona split is the answer."

The proposal is simple: continue positioning for "builders" but start selling specifically to each. Map our seven products cleanly to the two personas. Build distinct onboarding, packaging, and messaging for each. Then use the shared platform underneath as the enterprise consolidation story.
